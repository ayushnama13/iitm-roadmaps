## Roadmap

1. Complexity, order notation, master theorem, NP-hardness.
2. Divide and conquer (FFT); dynamic programming (LCS, Viterbi, backprop view).
3. Randomised algorithms and graph algorithms.

## What your IITM course sheet says

### From the PDF: DA2303

**Official description:** The students should be able to  1. Identify if a given algorithmic problem has a structure that makes it solvable.  2. Systematically analyse the complexity class of algorithms, and grasp concepts of computational hardness, i.e.  NP completeness etc.  3. Identify the conditions for applying a divide-conquer, greedy, dynamic programming paradigms to problems,  4. Use the above-mentioned paradigms to develop algorithms for specific problems.  5. Deconstruct data-science problems into their fundamental computational components and build algorithms for  the same.

**Official syllabus (trimmed):**

> Course Contents:
>  1. Basics. (1 week) Computational complexity, order notation, master theorem, NP-hardness, worst-case and
>  average
>  case complexity.
>  2. Basic Paradigms (3 weeks) Incremental design (insertion sort), decremental design (GCD, factorial). Divide and
>  Conquer: Recurrences, master theorem, merge-sort, Fast-Fourier transform,
>  3. Dynamic Programming (3 weeks) Optimal substructure,, Recurrences, Invariants, Memoization, Recursion
>  without memoization, Tabulation. Examples: Longest common subsequence, Viterbi algorithm for HMM inference,
>  Backpropagation for gradient computation in Neural networks.
>  4. Greedy Methods (3 weeks) Greedy choice, local optimality. Examples where it is optimal: Huffman encoding,
>  Dijkstra’s shortest path. Other examples: Gradient descent, sub-modular maximization.
>  5. Advanced Topics (3 weeks) Continuous optimization algorithms: gradient descent, convexity, eigen-value prob-
>  lems. Randomized algorithms: Sampling using MCMC, approximate SVD, matrix multiplication, random
>  projections, locality sensitive hashing.

**Official textbooks:** 1. Cormen, Leiserson, Rivest and Stein. Introduction to Algorithms by MIT Press, Third Edition, 2009.  2. Christopher Bishop. Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning. Springer.  3. Rajeev Motwani and Prabhakar Raghavan. Randomized Algorithms. Cambridge University Press.
